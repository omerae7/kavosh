# Scientific Research

## Formulate the Question

Specify the population or subject, intervention or exposure, comparison, outcome, and time horizon when relevant. Identify the question type: effectiveness, safety, diagnosis, prognosis, prevalence, mechanism, or causation.

## Select the Evidence

Match the evidence type to the question:

- current authoritative guidance for practical recommendations;
- systematic reviews or meta-analyses for synthesizing multiple similar studies;
- original studies for detail, new data, or examination of synthesis limitations;
- controlled trials for many intervention questions;
- observational studies for prevalence, prognosis, rare harms, or exposures that cannot be experimentally assigned;
- laboratory or mechanistic research for biological explanation, not automatic proof of clinical effect.

Do not apply one evidence hierarchy to every question. Prioritize the fit between study design and question over the study label.

## Evaluate the Study

Examine:

- design and preregistration when relevant;
- sample size, characteristics, and representativeness;
- comparison group and allocation method;
- definitions of exposure, intervention, and outcome;
- follow-up duration;
- attrition and missing data;
- effect size and uncertainty interval;
- clinical importance versus statistical significance;
- prespecified versus exploratory analyses;
- limitations, funding, and conflicts of interest;
- and applicability to the population or setting in question.

Do not present one study as scientific consensus. Do not count multiple studies sharing data or a research team as fully independent evidence.

## Synthesize the Evidence

Examine consistency in direction and magnitude, population differences, heterogeneity, methodological quality, and possible publication bias. If a review or meta-analysis combines inconsistent or weak studies, do not accept the pooled estimate without explaining evidence quality.

## Medical and Health Claims

For a recommendation with health consequences, examine current authoritative guidance, warnings from responsible bodies, individual conditions, and evidence limitations. Do not present general information as a personalized diagnosis or prescription. In an emergency or immediate-risk situation, state the safety priority clearly.

For a cause-and-effect claim, also read [Causal Analysis](causal-analysis.md).
