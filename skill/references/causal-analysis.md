# Causal Analysis

## Define the Claim

Specify the proposed cause, outcome, population, time, and conditions. First determine whether the source reports only an association or was genuinely designed to support causal inference.

## Core Tests

- **Temporal order:** The proposed cause must occur before the outcome.
- **Confounding:** A third variable may explain both the proposed cause and the outcome.
- **Reverse causality:** The outcome may have influenced the proposed causal factor.
- **Selection and measurement bias:** Sample entry or variable measurement may create an artificial relationship.
- **Mechanism:** A plausible explanation may exist, but a mechanism alone does not prove causation.
- **Dose-response relationship:** Examine it when available, but do not treat its presence or absence as decisive by itself.
- **Alternative evidence:** Search for competing explanations and contrary data.
- **Replication:** Examine consistency across different populations, methods, and time periods.

## Design Strength

Evaluate whether the study design can answer a causal question. Randomized trials, natural experiments, quasi-experimental methods, longitudinal studies, cross-sectional studies, and case reports do not have equal causal strength. Do not treat statistical adjustment as complete elimination of confounding.

## Conclusion Language

Use language proportional to the evidence:

- The evidence supports a causal relationship.
- The evidence is consistent with a causal relationship but does not establish it.
- Only an association has been observed; the evidence is insufficient for causation.
- Current evidence is uncertain or conflicting.
- Reliable evidence is inconsistent with the causal claim.

Do not transform “is associated with,” “may,” or “increased alongside” into “causes.”

## Conditions That Could Change the Conclusion

Identify what additional data, stronger design, better control of confounding, or independent replication could change the causal assessment.
