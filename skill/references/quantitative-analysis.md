# Quantitative Analysis, Statistics, Scenarios, and Sensitivity

## Input Control

Before calculating, record:

- the origin of every number;
- unit and currency;
- time range and reference date;
- population, sample, or denominator;
- metric definition;
- whether the data is observed, user-provided, or calculated;
- and measurement precision and limitations.

Do not mix percentages, rates, counts, amounts, averages, and probabilities. Independently recheck component totals, unit conversions, and decisive calculations.

## Calculation and Reporting

Show the formula or method to the extent required for review. Connect every result to its inputs and do not report more precision than the data supports. When inputs conflict, do not choose one without justification before calculating.

Do not equate statistical significance with practical importance or effect size. When available, report uncertainty intervals, sample size, and methodological limitations alongside the result.

## Probability

Provide a probability only when it is grounded in one of the following:

- valid and relevant frequency data;
- an explainable statistical model;
- a credible study or source;
- or a reviewable calculation from explicit inputs.

Do not treat the percentage of agreeing sources, the number of user reports, or subjective impressions as probability. When the basis is insufficient, write **cannot be estimated reliably** and, where possible, explain risk-increasing and risk-reducing factors qualitatively.

## Scenario and Sensitivity Analysis

Initially use only data, ranges, or numeric assumptions supplied by the user or a verified source. If an important numeric input is missing, do not invent a number or range. Present a formula, variable, break-even point, or required data instead.

Use sample numbers only when the user explicitly requests them. Label them **illustrative only—not an estimate**, and do not use them as the basis for a forecast, probability, recommendation, or final conclusion.

For sensitivity analysis:

1. identify the decisive variables;
2. obtain each valid range from a source or the user;
3. find the point at which the conclusion changes;
4. separate robust findings from fragile findings;
5. examine simultaneous changes only with a clear basis.

## Scoring

If a numeric score is necessary, define criteria, weights, and the calculation method before the result. Do not hide arbitrary weighting. Do not present an evidence-quality score as the probability that a conclusion is correct. When no valid method exists, use a reasoned qualitative assessment.
